\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Objective.} Identify documented flight computers, sensors, recovery electronics, scientific payload missions and validation practices in student competition rockets within a measured or nominal 3-km apogee class, and map that evidence to RTC 2027 MDR claims. \textbf{Search.} The user's first broad Web of Science Topic strategy combined rocket/launch-vehicle/missile terms with avionics, navigation and telemetry terms; exports were labelled 1609 Web of Science and 788 IEEE Xplore records. \textbf{Methods.} The unique supplied files contained 2396 parsable BibTeX records (1609 WOS and 787 IEEE); 75 DOI/title duplicates were removed, leaving 2321 unique records. A documented broad title/abstract filter yielded 1148 candidates; 121 met a prioritisation score. Supplementary student-team technical reports, theses, CanSat mission guidance and design reviews were also assessed separately from the database denominator. \textbf{Results.} Seven competition/competition-linked examples and seven contextual avionics studies were examined, including two papers for which the user subsequently provided licensed full texts. AVES/AVES II anchor the 3-km avionics precedent, while a supplementary academic-first payload search documents scientific missions ranging from thermal/structural sensing and fluid dynamics to biomedical studies, imaging and CubeSat technology demonstration; no source establishes Pico superiority or verifies the proposed RTC thermal mission. Graphs show the screening profile, bibliographic topic signals, evidence classes and a primary reported hardware inventory; a separate battery curve is clearly labelled as an engineering model. \textbf{Limitations.} This is a rapid systematic \emph{scoping} review, not a completed PRISMA systematic review: most candidates were not independently full-text screened, and nominal target altitude is not necessarily achieved apogee. \textbf{Conclusion.} The evidence supports a modular sensing, logging and redundant-recovery design \emph{concept}, while actual Pico flight readiness must be established through simulation and test.
\end{abstract}

\section{Review question and scope}
\textbf{Question:} What avionics designs and science/payload mission concepts have been documented in student rocketry competitions with nominal apogees at or near 3 km, and what validation evidence exists for their use?

\textbf{Apogee eligibility is explicitly classified:} \textbf{A} = measured apogee $\leq3000$ m; \textbf{B} = nominal 3000 m competition class (actual flight apogee unspecified); \textbf{C} = a documented lower-altitude competition (actual individual flight apogee unknown); and \textbf{D} = relevant sounding-rocket research lacking sufficient altitude or competition evidence, retained only for contextual synthesis. In the 2026/2027 RTC ``3K'' category, the nominal goal is at least 3 km; that categorisation does not imply every entry's actual apogee was exactly or below 3000 m \cite{rtc2027rules}. We include the 10,000-ft (3048-m) IREC class as a \emph{near-3-km adjacent competition stratum}, not as a measured apogee $\le3000$ m.


\section{Methods}
\subsection{Review design, eligibility and research questions}
This is a \emph{rapid, systematic scoping evidence review}, not a completed two-reviewer systematic review or meta-analysis. It asks which flight computers, sensors, recovery-control arrangements, power systems, data logging and telemetry architectures were \emph{documented} for student competition rockets whose nominal class or measured apogee is at or near 3 km. A further question is which directly documented design features can support RTC 2027 MDR requirements, rather than which commercial board is ``best.'' The review uses PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-S as \emph{reporting guidance}, while disclosing incomplete screening and the absence of a prospectively registered protocol \cite{page2021,rethlefsen2021}.

\textbf{Eligibility.} Include as primary evidence: a named student competition/team, a measured flight altitude $\leq3000$ m, credible nominal 3-km class, documented lower-altitude event, or a 10,000-ft adjacent class, and at least one citable avionics design feature or outcome. Class A: measured flight $\leq3000$ m; B: nominal 3-km class with unconfirmed measured apogee; C: documented lower-altitude competition context; D: informative rocketry research without the required competition/altitude link (context only). Class E is an adjacent 10,000-ft (3048-m) IREC target category, which is only 1.6\% above 3 km and is analysed separately, never relabelled as a $\le3000$-m flight. Preflight technical reports are not proof of flight success. Exclude from primary cases orbital launchers, general military missiles, unrelated aircraft/UAV systems, and avionics papers with no competition/altitude evidence.

\subsection{First search: databases, exact strategy and export counts}
The \textbf{first supplied search} was deliberately sensitive and broad; the original Web of Science Topic expression is reproduced verbatim below (line breaks added only for typesetting):
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
TS=((rocket* OR "launch vehicle*" OR "sounding rocket*" OR missile*)\newline
AND (avionics OR "flight computer*" OR "guidance system*"\newline
OR "navigation system*" OR "flight control system*"\newline
OR telemetry OR "attitude determination"))
\end{quote}
This initial search yielded the user's stated \textbf{1609 Web of Science} and \textbf{788 IEEE Xplore} records. The field tag \texttt{TS=} belongs to Web of Science; the precise IEEE Xplore interface-specific field translation/limits were \emph{not preserved} in the uploaded metadata, so no exact IEEE query is invented here. These counts are database-search \emph{labels}, not assertions that every record pertains to competition rocketry. The Web of Science export carries 3 November 2026 retrieval metadata; the IEEE export is labelled as the November 2026 collection. No unrecorded date/language limits are presumed. A complete future review should also search Scopus, aerospace conference repositories, EuRoC and IREC team reports, university theses and official competition reports with a separate competition-specific strategy. Such additional retrieval has not been counted as part of the original database-search totals.

\subsection{Bibliographic processing and transparent screening}
Three uploaded files were inspected: \texttt{Combined 1609.bib}, \texttt{Combined IEEE 788.bib} and \texttt{Combined IEEE 788(1).bib}. The two IEEE files have identical SHA-256 hashes, so only one was processed. The Web of Science file contains 1609 parsable entry starts; the IEEE file contains \textbf{787 actual BibTeX entry starts} despite ``788'' in its filename (a further \texttt{@} is part of a conference title). The processing sample is thus $1609+787=\mathbf{2396}$ parsed records. Titles, abstracts, source labels, year and DOI were extracted. Deduplication preferentially used normalised DOI, otherwise a lower-case, punctuation-stripped title. This removed \textbf{75} records and retained \textbf{2321} unique bibliographic records.

The post-export \emph{computational broad-screen proxy} required both a rocket-related expression and an electronics-related expression within combined title and abstract fields. The exact case-insensitive Python regular expressions are archived in the accompanying screening script; the term stems are also shown below.
\begin{quote}\footnotesize
\textbf{Rocket set:} rocket, sounding, launch vehicle, IREC, EuRoC, TEKNOFEST, spaceport.\\
\textbf{Electronics set:} avionics, flight computer, electronics, telemetry, data logging, parachute, recovery, apogee, flight control, sensor, attitude, control system, navigation, payload, onboard, embedded, altimeter, GPS, tracking.
\end{quote} Exactly \textbf{1148} matched; \textbf{1173} were non-matches. Among matches, an additive metadata heuristic (3 points for competition/student keywords in the title, 2 for avionics-related title terms, 2 for competition keywords in the abstract, 2 for altitude/apogee mentions in combined title/abstract; score threshold $\ge5$ of 9 possible points) identified \textbf{121 higher-priority candidates} (score $\geq5$). \textbf{This was not a comprehensive full-text screening of 121 studies}; the remaining 1027 broad candidates were not individually assessed in full.

Primary competition reports and institutional theses/webpages were additionally found through targeted searches and explicitly labelled as \emph{supplementary sources}, not silently counted inside the 2321 indexed records. Seven competition or competition-linked cases were extracted for the descriptive evidence table; seven further studies were retained as secondary contextual evidence. The seven are not the final included sample from a complete PRISMA screening, nor evidence of the population prevalence of any hardware model.


\subsection{Focused supplementary search: payload missions and full-text access (5 November 2026)}
A second, \emph{targeted and separately tracked} public-source search was added because the original avionics keyword strategy was not designed to recover scientific payload studies. This expanded stage prioritised peer-reviewed academic papers and university repositories, next university capstone/technical reports and student-team primary pages, and lastly competition press reports. It searched for 3-km EuRoC, IREC 10,000-ft and RTC 3K cases, requiring identifiable mission objectives and separating flight-carried hardware from completed scientific outcomes. Queries combined \texttt{EuRoC}, \texttt{IREC}, \texttt{student rocket}, \texttt{CanSat}, \texttt{payload mission}, \texttt{temperature}, \texttt{pressure}, \texttt{imaging}, \texttt{experiment}, and \texttt{full text/PDF}. Searches were conducted in public search-engine results and checked against institutional repositories, university-team reports, open journals and competition document archives. This is a \emph{purposeful supplemental evidence stream}, not an exhaustive new database search; its results were not added to the 2321-record denominator, and no invented PRISMA retrieval count is assigned to it.

Eligibility for \emph{direct payload precedent} required a named rocket-team or competition technical report and a describable mission objective. \emph{Adjacent payload precedent} includes CanSat guidance and team CDRs; these do not demonstrate 3-km rocket-flight performance. Peer-reviewed sounding-rocket electronics papers with no competition/altitude verification remain contextual. We classify access as \textbf{F} (full text actually read), \textbf{A} (abstract/metadata only), or \textbf{U} (full text not currently retrievable). A report's proposed device and mission are not equivalent to a verified flight outcome. For licensed full texts supplied by the user, F means the PDF content was read for evidence extraction; it does not mean the paper is open-access. Data extraction records mission goal, instruments, power/data interfaces, claimed mission outcome, altitude basis, and applicability to RTC Appendix 03, Section 7.1 \cite{rtc2027rules}.

\subsection{Data extraction, figure coding and appraisal}
The extraction fields were source/venue, competition, nominal versus measured apogee, MCU, inertial/pressure sensing, telemetry/GNSS, persistent data storage, recovery path, available testing/outcome evidence and limitations. Reliability class \textbf{R1} denotes a primary technical report or thesis, \textbf{R2} an experimental/HIL research paper, \textbf{R3} an institutional story, and \textbf{R4} abstract-only metadata. Inconsistently reported or absent items were treated as \emph{not disclosed}, not as absent hardware. No estimates of failure rates or processor ``win rates'' were made.

All graphs report their denominator and evidence basis. Workflow and year counts come from the two unique metadata exports. Figure~\ref{fig:topics} counts \emph{non-mutually-exclusive regular-expression signals}, not confirmed use of components in flight, and may include military/large launch-vehicle papers because the first search explicitly included \texttt{missile*}. Figure~\ref{fig:classes} summarises only the seven manually extracted competition cases. Figure~\ref{fig:aves2} uses an identifiable published component inventory in the \emph{AVES II} technical report. A separate analytic power model (Fig.~\ref{fig:power}) is marked as \emph{calculated engineering assumption}, not a flight measurement or an outcome extracted from the review \cite{astg2022aves2,rpicopico,rtc2027rules}.

\subsection{Reproducibility and departures from a complete SLR}
The accompanying package contains a screening script, the source-record audit and figure data tables (CSV). To independently reproduce the initial numbers, use the \emph{original} two BibTeX exports: the ZIP does not redistribute those large, user-supplied libraries. The included seven-case charts describe the original core sample; additional targeted 10,000-ft examples below are NOT part of that seven-case denominator. The current narrative/graphs represent an honest rapid evidence review; a journal-grade systematic review would require independently screened full texts for all potentially eligible records, exclusion reasons, search-platform settings, a registered protocol or predeclared amendments, and a completed PRISMA flow \cite{page2021,rethlefsen2021}.

\subsection{Interpreting the primary competition evidence}
\textbf{Convergent functions:} (i) pressure and inertial sensing for flight phase recognition; (ii) logging independent of ground communications; (iii) parachute deployment control separated from at least some non-safety subsystems; (iv) telemetry or GPS for recovery and post-flight analysis; (v) explicit ground or simulation verification. The best documented 3-km team reports are AVES and AVES II; the expanded payload cases are a distinct purposive sample and not evidence that their exact parts are universally optimal \cite{astg2021aves,astg2022aves2}.

\textbf{Architectural diversity:} 3-km AVES II uses a custom high-performance STM32 MCU plus separate COTS electronics; the Czech challenge thesis documents an STM32-plus-supercapacitor arrangement; the RTC Stargazer report demonstrates an actual near-3-km flight but does not expose its part numbers. Consequently, there is no defensible numerical frequency table of microcontroller brands for all 3-km competition finalists \cite{astg2022aves2,zapadlo2023,stargazer2026}.

\textbf{Recovery-state example.} The avionics paper describes a main apogee decision after an altitude fall of about 10 m from peak. \subsection{Companion recovery paper: what ``cold gas'' actually means}
The recovery paper's Sections III--V and Figs.~1--6 describe (i) a payload that separates near apogee with its own parachute and electronic tracking, and (ii) rocket drogue/main deployments controlled via primary/backup flight computers and MOSFET-switched initiation circuitry \cite{bakirci2022recovery}. The authors compare springs, solenoids, hot-gas and cold-gas methods, then describe an assembly that combines \emph{compressed CO$_2$ cartridges with black-powder/e-match initiation}. It is therefore inaccurate to cite the paper as a demonstration of a purely mechanical or non-pyrotechnic spring latch. The paper states that the release circuits and communication system were tested, but does not present a measured competition flight outcome, deployment reliability statistic or RTC regulatory acceptance.

\textbf{Power and integration caveat.} The recovery paper describes parallel Sony VTC6 cells for the payload computer and a separate higher-voltage release supply, while the avionics paper discusses alkaline 9-V configurations. These statements describe different roles/sections and are not a single verified power architecture. This review does not combine them into a claimed flight-tested battery configuration. The recovery paper's separate payload parachute and energetic deployment are also \emph{not automatically allowed} under RTC Appendix 03, Section 7.1; that rule's payload retention and stored-energy limitations must be checked independently \cite{rtc2027rules}.

\textbf{A critical microgravity caveat.} A ~3-km rocket coasting ballistically does \emph{not} automatically provide the long-duration, low-disturbance microgravity environment obtained by dedicated high-altitude sounding rockets. Drag, spin and vibration alter specific force. A project labelled ``microgravity'' should report its measured acceleration/rotation environment before claiming a controlled microgravity exposure \cite{moura2024mgx,umd2022terpulence}.

\subsection{Implications for the current proposed thermal payload}
The documented peer designs offer stronger experimental comparisons than simply logging two temperatures. For the RTC Pico concept, a feasible \textbf{baseline} remains two calibrated, retained DS18B20 probes (avionics bay versus inner airframe wall) with phase-aligned data and uncertainty estimates. The new review suggests two optional, still team-proposed refinements: (i) compare the temperature difference to an independently measured acceleration/pressure history, and (ii) report a measured thermal response lag to a controlled preflight step input. These additions preserve relatively low mass/power and make a testable \emph{engineering-science objective}, rather than a second instance of standard rocket telemetry \cite{ukcansat2024,rtc2027rules}.

A more distinctive yet still plausible alternative is \textbf{structural strain or vibration measurement}, motivated by TUDSaT's smart fins, if the structures team can safely mount and calibrate gauges without affecting flight integrity. The fluid, biological and deployable missions have real university precedent but need substantially more verification and cannot be treated as inexpensive, RTC-compliant substitutions without explicit systems/safety review.

\textbf{Recommended payload MDR evidence package:} a one-sentence scientific question, a null/comparison condition, a measurement diagram, sensor range/accuracy, expected operating timeline, mass and power budget, retention/containment plan, measurable full/partial/failure criteria, planned ground verification and a specified method for recovering data after splashdown. The organiser's GPS beacon is distinct from the additional payload \cite{rtc2027rules,rtc2027mdr}.

\subsection{Limits of the expanded payload search}
The evidence set includes one peer-reviewed IAC abstract and a peer-reviewed platform-methodology paper, one university repository technical report, university capstone/team narratives, and secondary competition reporting. A few teams report recovered hardware but not raw data or analysed experimental outcomes. No pooled payload success rate or quantitative ``best mission'' ranking is warranted. The supplied Bakirci--Ozer full texts provide specific electronics/recovery hardware designs, but do not specify a completed scientific payload experiment or a verified competition flight. The new cases are reported separately from the original seven-case avionics figures and the 2321 database-record denominator; their discovery does not imply that all possible near-3-km payload studies were found. The case studies provide \emph{design possibilities and practical failure lessons}, not flight qualification of the proposed RTC equipment.

\subsection{Quantitative engineering check: proposed logger power and storage (model, not flight evidence)}
The reviewed competition studies support the \emph{architecture and functions}, not a numerical Pico endurance guarantee. To make the RTC electrical-endurance claim testable, a separate, explicitly \emph{assumption-based} preliminary model uses a nominal 3.7-V, 2.6-Ah cell ($9.62$ Wh), $85\%$ converter efficiency, and $3.3$-V subsystem operating voltage. With a constant average current $I$ (amperes), predicted runtime is
\[
 t_{\mathrm{ideal}}(I)=\frac{3.7\,(2.6)\,(0.85)}{3.3\,I}\;\mathrm{hours}.
\]
The team design proposal assumes an average logger load near 56 mA but conservatively sizes around 200 mA; the predicted duration at 200 mA is about 12.39 h. This \emph{is not a measured runtime}: battery capacity derating, cut-off voltage, peak SD-card write current, protection losses, temperature, ageing and release loads must be tested. The RTC's $>1.5$ h requirement applies to the actual assembled system \cite{rtc2027rules,rpicopico}.

The 10,000-ft IREC category is an especially relevant comparator to RTC 3K because $10,000\,\mathrm{ft}\times0.3048=3048\,\mathrm{m}$, only 48 m (1.6\%) above a nominal 3,000 m target. To avoid changing the original seven-case descriptive figures without rerunning the complete search, this additional stratum is presented \emph{separately}, not added to Figures~\ref{fig:classes} or~\ref{fig:aves2}. \section{Evidence quality, limitations and conclusions}
\textbf{Evidence quality.} Two team reports provide unusually rich 3-km detail but were prepared by the same organisation in successive years. One university thesis provides a 3-km design connection but not a verified flight of its new computer. Lower-altitude case studies are useful for power, telemetry and design development but operate in different loads and mission envelopes. A university news report gives actual near-3-km apogee with almost no reproducible avionics BOM. The two full-text Bakirci--Ozer studies provide schematics and reported ground/integration tests at a 10,000-ft design target, but are not independent qualifying competition-flight outcomes. These weaknesses preclude a pooled success rate, failure probability or ranking of processors.

\textbf{Review limitations.} (1) A single reviewer applied the screening workflow; (2) 1027 broad candidate records were not all individually fully screened; (3) no protocol was prospectively registered; (4) publication and outcome reporting are highly variable; (5) multiple primary technical reports were published pre-flight, while flight outcomes appeared separately; (6) published web documentation can change; (7) nominal 3-km target is not the same as measured apogee $\leq3000$ m. To upgrade this work to a formal systematic review, independently screen all 2321 records (or a pre-registered refined search with documented exclusions), retrieve all eligible full texts, and provide a complete PRISMA flow and critical appraisal \cite{page2021,rethlefsen2021}.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Public evidence for approximately 3-km student competition avionics favours modular MCU-based design, calibrated barometric/inertial sensing, flight logging independent of telemetry, well-defined recovery redundancy and extensive ground verification. The expanded payload evidence also shows that student teams have undertaken environmental sensing, live imaging, strain measurement, liquid sloshing and capillarity, biological/biomedical experiments, GNSS/SDR demonstrations and autonomous technology demonstrations; the reported outcomes vary greatly in evidentiary strength. The evidence does \emph{not} establish any particular board or payload mission as universally optimal. The newly accessible Bakirci--Ozer full texts strengthen the case for distributed avionics, separate sensing and logging, while offering no quantitative evidence that any MCU is universally superior \cite{bakirci2022,bakirci2022recovery}. A Raspberry Pi Pico architecture can be defended in the RTC MDR as an economical preliminary design, provided the team specifies and eventually demonstrates its timing, sensor range, fault isolation, recovery authority, power margin and flight-data integrity against RTC requirements.
